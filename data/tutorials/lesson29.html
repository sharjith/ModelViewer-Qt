Lesson 29: Ray Tracing in Practice

Introduction

Ray tracing follows light through the scene to produce reflections, soft shadows, glass and realistic lighting that the real-time viewer can only approximate. ModelViewer has two ray-tracing engines and uses the same materials and environment you already set up. This lesson takes you from a live ray-traced view to a high-resolution image on disk and a batch of views rendered in one go.

Two engines: the CPU engine (Embree) runs everywhere; the GPU engine (NVIDIA OptiX) needs an NVIDIA graphics card and a build with OptiX. Help → About tells you whether the GPU engine is enabled in your build. Simulation results and 3D plots are not drawn by the ray tracer.

A Live Ray-Traced View

Step 1: Prepare a scene
Open a model with interesting materials - the Futuristic Transport Shuttle or the Tea-Pot - and set up its environment and lights as in Lesson 10.
Step 2: Switch the rendering mode
Choose View → Rendering Mode → Ray Traced. The viewport starts rendering progressively: the image is noisy at first and settles as samples accumulate, then a denoiser cleans it up. Move the camera and it restarts.
Step 3: Compare with PBR
Switch between PBR (Metallic-Roughness) and Ray Traced. Materials, environment lighting and the shadow-catcher floor follow the raster viewer, so you see the same scene, now with true reflections and shadows.

The Ray Tracing Dialog

Visualization → Ray Tracing... opens the settings and the offline render. It has three tabs.

TabWhat it holds
BasicRender Engine (Auto prefers OptiX when available, Embree (CPU) or OptiX (GPU)), Max Samples per pixel, Max Bounces of light per path, Enable Denoiser and its Denoiser Engine (Auto, OIDN on CPU, OIDN on GPU/CUDA or OptiX), and the Export Preset and Export Resolution with Match Viewport.
AdvancedFirefly Clamp Threshold (caps single bright samples), Max Transmission Bounces for glass, Russian Roulette Start Depth, Max Shadow Ray Hits (CPU only), Max Volume Scatter Bounces, Environment Importance Sampling, and the switches Shadows and Self Shadows.
DiagnosticsRead-only information about the renderer and the GPU, the traversal and denoiser in use, the resolution, the triangle count, the acceleration-structure build times, samples per second, MRays per second and the render time.

For a quicker preview lower Max Samples and Max Bounces; for a clean final image raise Max Samples, keep the denoiser on, and leave Environment Importance Sampling on to cut noise from bright skies. Hover over a setting for a short explanation.

Rendering and Exporting an Image

Step 1: Choose the size
Pick an Export Preset or type a width and height under Export Resolution, or click Match Viewport. A resolution larger than the viewport triggers an offline render, which may take a while and can be cancelled with the button that replaces Stop.
Step 2: Render
Click Render. The status line and the progress show how far it is; Stop ends it early.
Step 3: Save the image
Click Export Image... and choose PNG, JPEG, BMP, TIFF or OpenEXR. OpenEXR keeps the full dynamic range, which is what you want for further editing.
Screenshot: tutorial_29_rendered_image.png (700x450)
A finished high-resolution ray-traced image of the model
With an orthographic camera and a thin-walled glass material every pixel samples the same environment direction, so that glass renders flat. This is expected, not a bug - switch to a perspective projection (Lesson 6) for realistic glass. The dialog shows a warning when it detects this combination.

Batch Rendering Views

Tools → Batch Render Views... renders the camera views you captured in the Cameras panel to a folder with the ray tracer, in one run: tick the views, pick the format (PNG, JPEG, BMP, TIFF or OpenEXR) and the resolution, choose the folder and start. Combine it with scene states (Lesson 28) to produce a whole set of presentation images - overview, cutaway, details - without touching the model again.

Screenshot: tutorial_29_batch_render.png (600x480)
The Batch Render Views dialog with three captured views ticked
Remember the limits from earlier lessons: simulation results and 3D plots are drawn the same flat way in every mode. Use ray tracing for models, assemblies and presentation images, and the normal viewer for the scientific views.
